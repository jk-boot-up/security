ONE. Registering a passkey: the bank keeps only a public key.
  the bank sends a challenge of 32 random bytes and its relying party id bank.example
  ana's phone makes a new P-256 key pair for bank.example, keeps the private key, and returns a credential id of 16 bytes and a public key of 91 bytes
  the bank checks the challenge, the type, the origin and the relying party id hash: accepted (passkey ana's phone registered)
  what the bank stores for ana's passkey: a credential id, a 91-byte public key and a counter of 0, and nothing that can sign
  a copy of the bank's records cannot sign: an answer made with another key under ana's credential id: refused (signature does not match)
TWO. Signing in: a fresh challenge, signed once.
  ana touches her phone; it signs 37 bytes of authenticator data and the SHA-256 of the client data
  the bank checks challenge, type, origin, relying party id hash, flags, signature and counter: accepted (signed in as ana with ana's phone)
  the same answer sent again: refused (unknown or already used challenge)
  an answer that arrives 301 seconds after its challenge: refused (challenge expired)
  the same kind of answer with the user-verified flag switched on after signing: refused (signature does not match)
THREE. A look-alike site gets nothing it can use.
  ana opens https://bank-example.test, a copy of the bank's page
  it asks for a passkey for its own name bank-example.test: nothing (the authenticator has no passkey for bank-example.test)
  it passes on the bank's real challenge and asks for bank.example: nothing (the browser will not use bank.example for a page at https://bank-example.test)
  even a browser that skipped that rule writes the page's real origin, and the bank checks it: refused (origin https://bank-example.test is not https://bank.example)
  the same page asking for ana's password, which she types in: the password works at the bank: accepted (signed in as ana with a password)  <- A PASSWORD CAN BE PHISHED, A PASSKEY CANNOT
FOUR. User verification for payments, and counters against copies.
  a payment of 900 with only a touch: refused (user verification required)
  the same payment with her fingerprint as well: accepted (signed in as ana with ana's phone)
  ana's phone counts its signatures; the bank has now seen counter 6
  an attacker copies the phone's key; the real phone signs in first: accepted (signed in as ana with ana's phone)
  then the copy signs in: refused (counter 7 is not above 7: possible cloned authenticator)
  the passkey is now suspended until ana confirms it, even for the real phone: refused (passkey suspended after a counter went backwards)
  a synced passkey, counter 0: accepted (signed in as ana with ana's synced passkey)
  a synced passkey, counter 0: accepted (signed in as ana with ana's synced passkey)
  a synced passkey always reports 0, so a copy cannot be spotted by its counter: its protection is the account it syncs through
FIVE. The bill: recovery, lost devices and the move away from passwords.
  ben registers 2 passkeys, his phone and a security key, and keeps 1 recovery code of 20 characters
  ben's phone is lost; his new laptop has no passkey yet: nothing (the authenticator has no passkey for bank.example)
  the security key from his drawer: accepted (signed in as ben with ben's security key)
  the recovery code, once: accepted (recovered ben; register a new passkey now)
  the recovery code, twice: refused (recovery code unknown or already used)
  recovery is now the weakest way in, so it needs the same care as the passkeys themselves
  ana's password still works alongside her passkeys: accepted (signed in as ana with a password)  <- THE WEAKEST WAY IN SETS THE LEVEL
  once ana has two passkeys and the bank switches her password off: refused (password sign-in is switched off for ana)
  a real bank uses a maintained WebAuthn library: the -with-spring-security version uses Spring Security's passkey support
