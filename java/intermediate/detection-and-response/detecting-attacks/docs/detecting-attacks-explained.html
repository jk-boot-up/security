Detecting Attacks in Logs, Explained

The idea in one sentence

Detection turns logs into a few alerts that people trust, so write each rule as a hypothesis about an attacker, measure it against a normal day, add context to remove the false positives, and review what it misses.

The 5 acts

1. One Monday of logs

The input is one synthetic Monday at an online bank with 200 accounts: 772 events, 768 logins of which 145 failed, and 4 grants of the admin role. Each line has a time, a type, an account, an address and a place, such as "07:58:18 login u042 10.2.0.43 London success". Hidden in it are four attacks, which only the scoring knows: A, password spraying, 80 events from 203.0.113.50 between 10:00 and 10:21; B, brute force, 31 events from 198.51.100.7 between 12:00 and 12:04; C, impossible travel, one login from Sydney at 15:25; and D, privilege change, u117 making itself an administrator at 10:40. 113 of the 772 events belong to an attack. The rest is normal, and some of it looks like an attack: typing mistakes, two people who forgot their password (6 failures each at 09:10), five VPN users who appear in New York and then Frankfurt, a phone placed in Frankfurt, a real traveller from Paris to New York, and a break-glass admin grant at 03:00 with no ticket.

2. Two attacks, worked out

The first input is attack A: every failure from 203.0.113.50, 79 failures on 40 accounts between 10:00:00 and 10:21:15. Counted per account, as the brute-force rule counts, the most failures one account has in 5 minutes is 2, under the threshold of 10, so brute force stays quiet. Counted per address, as the spraying rule counts: u101 at 10:00:00, u102 at 10:00:25, and so on to u108 at 10:02:55, which makes 8 different accounts in 20 minutes, and the spraying rule fires. The second input is attack C: u088's two successful logins, Madrid at 15:00:00 and Sydney (192.0.2.88) at 15:25:00. The speed is worked out: 17685 km in 25 minutes is 17685 x 60 / 25 = 42444 km/h, over the 900 km/h of an airliner, so impossible travel fires. The near miss is a real trip: u030 in Paris at 08:00 and New York at 17:00, 5837 km in 540 minutes, 5837 x 60 / 540 = 649 km/h, under 900, so no alert.

3. A first version of each rule

The input is five rules, each a guess about an attacker: brute-force(3), 3 failures for 1 account in 5 minutes; spraying(8), 8 accounts failing from 1 address in 20 minutes; impossible-travel, over 900 km/h and 500 km or more; privilege-change, the admin role granted by a non-approver or with no ticket; and success-after-failures, a success after 1 failure from a new address. The result, scored against the hidden attacks: 29 alerts, 8 true and 21 false, and all four attacks A, B, C and D are noticed. Impossible travel alone raises 14 alerts, 11 of them false; success-after-failures raises 9, 7 false. Each false alert is a look-alike: 10 from the VPN users, 5 typing mistakes, 2 + 2 forgotten passwords, 1 phone address and 1 break-glass grant. An analyst who gets 29 alerts a day, most of them false, soon stops reading them.

4. Tuning one threshold

The input is the normal day, without the attack events, measured the way the brute-force rule counts: the most failures one account has in 5 minutes. The worst are u010 and u011 with 6 (they forgot their password); next come four accounts with 2 (typing mistakes). The attack, B on u042 from 198.51.100.7, has 30 failures in 5 minutes, then a success at 12:04:10. The safe range is worked out: above the normal day means more than 6, so at least 7; not above the attack means at most 30. So any threshold from 7 to 30 works; 10 is chosen, with room on both sides. The sweep checks it: thresholds 3 and 5 give 3 alerts, 2 of them false; 10 and 25 give 1 alert, true; 40 gives no alert at all, and attack B is MISSED. A threshold is a measurement, not a guess. A slower attacker would stay under it, which is what the other rules are for.

5. The tuned set, and what it tells the analyst

The input is three changes: brute force needs 10 failures, impossible travel skips the bank VPN's address 198.18.0.1, and success-after-failures needs 5 failures and an address that is not one of the account's usual ones from earlier weeks. Spraying and privilege change stay as they were. The result: 9 alerts for the day, 7 true and 2 false, and all four attacks are noticed. In time order: the 03:00 privilege change for u060 (false), u033 at 08:20 (false), spraying from 203.0.113.50 at 10:02 (A), u117 from Singapore at 10:11 (A), u117 made admin at 10:40 (D), brute force on u042 at 12:01, its travel and its success at 12:04 (B), and u088 from Sydney at 15:25 (C). The two false ones are healthy: the break-glass grant at 03:00 should be reviewed anyway, and the phone's address placed in Frankfurt is a geolocation error. An analyst can read nine alerts.

The verdict

Start from the attacks that matter, write one rule for each, measure every rule on real logs before enabling it, keep allow-lists and thresholds owned and reviewed, send alerts to somebody who acts on them, and test the rules with replayed attacks.

How to recognise this in code you did not write

  • Sigma rules and SIEM correlation searches.
  • Cloud "impossible travel" and "unfamiliar sign-in" alerts.
  • ATT&CK technique IDs T1110 and T1078 in detection documentation.
  • Alert fatigue as a finding in an incident review.

Where you have already met this

  • SIEM correlation rules and "detection as code" repositories (Sigma rules).
  • Cloud sign-in alerts such as "unfamiliar sign-in properties" and "atypical travel".
  • Account lockout, which stops brute force but is itself a denial of service for spraying.
  • MITRE ATT&CK technique pages for brute force and valid accounts.
