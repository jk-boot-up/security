Risk Management, Explained

The idea in one sentence

A risk number without a range and a tail is a promise nobody can keep.

The 5 acts

1. One risk, from ranges to money

The input is an expert's range for how often ransomware hits the core banking system in a year (least 0.05, most likely 0.15, most 0.5) and for what one event costs (least $500k, most likely $3.0M, most $20.0M). The heat map would score it likelihood 2 x impact 5 = 10. The average of a triangle range is (least + most likely + most) / 3: 0.233 events a year and $7.8M an event, so the expected loss is 0.233 x $7.8M = $1.8M a year. One simulated year takes three draws between 0 and 1. In year 11, the first draw 0.853 is past the most likely point 0.222, so the rate is 0.5 - sqrt(0.147 x 0.45 x 0.35) = 0.348 events a year. The second draw 0.737 lies between P(none) = e^-0.348 = 0.706 and P(at most 1) = 0.952, so the year has one event. The third draw 0.126 reads the loss range at $500k + sqrt(0.126 x $19.5M x $2.5M) = $3.0M. The result of 50,000 such years: 79.7% have no ransomware, the expected loss is $1.8M a year, and 1 year in 10 loses more than $8.0M.

2. The register: the heat map against money

The input is the register: eight risks of a bank, each with a heat map score (likelihood x impact, 1 to 5 each) and two ranges. Card breach and insider fraud score 12, ransomware only 10, because its likelihood is low. The result of 50,000 simulated years: ransomware loses $1.8M a year on average, third-party breach $1.0M and card breach $920k, while the outage, scored 10, loses only $444k. The heat map's top three are card-breach, insider-fraud and outage; the money's top three are ransomware, third-party and card-breach. Only card-breach is in both lists.

3. The whole register: the bad years

The input is the same register, added up in each of the 50,000 years. The result is a spread: 22.0% of years lose $0 to $2M, 27.1% $2M to $4M, and a long tail, with 2.6% of years losing $20M or more. The expected loss is $5.8M a year, and the eight averages (mean events x mean loss) add to the same $5.8M, which checks the simulation. The tail is the news: 1 year in 10 loses more than $12.8M, 1 in 20 more than $16.8M, and 41.4% of years lose more than $5.0M. With 1,000 years instead of 50,000 the estimate is 0.9% away. Which input matters: raising a most likely loss by 50% moves the expected loss by the events a year times the change, divided by three. For ransomware 0.233 x $1.5M / 3 = $117k; the simulation gives +$116k.

4. Controls within a budget

The input is seven controls, each with a cost a year and factors on the risks it touches: offline backups keep 0.3 of a ransomware loss, multi-factor login keeps 0.4 of the phishing events and 0.8 of the ransomware events, and so on. Each control alone avoids some loss: backups avoid $1.3M for $250k, a net benefit of $1.0M; endpoint detection avoids $1.1M for $400k. There are 2^7 - 1 = 127 sets of controls, and 56 of them cost at most $800k. Each is scored on the same 50,000 years, so the difference between two sets is the controls, not the luck. The best set is backups, mfa, vendors and approval: cost $610k, expected loss $5.8M -> $3.1M, net benefit $2.7M - $610k = $2.1M. Cheapest first takes approval, mfa, vendors and scrubbing: cost $560k, expected loss $3.8M, net benefit $1.5M. The best set gains $581k a year more.

5. The appetite, and what is left

The input is the appetite, the board's number: at most 5.0% of years may lose more than $5.0M. Before any control, 41.4% of years do: outside. After the best set within the budget, 19.5%: still outside, with a residual expected loss of $3.1M and 1 year in 20 losing more than $8.4M. With all seven controls, costing $1.7M, 4.1%: within. Trying every set for the cheapest one within the appetite gives all seven controls: no smaller set is enough, and it costs $910k more than the budget. The decision is the board's: spend more, insure the rest, or change the appetite. What the model cannot know: the ranges are an expert's guesses, the risks are independent here while real incidents cluster, and an incident nobody imagined is not in the register.

The verdict

Keep the register as ranges, simulate, rank by expected loss and by the tail, price controls against the loss they avoid, state the appetite as a number, and show the model's limits with the result.

How to recognise this in code you did not write

  • A heat map with no money on it.
  • A control budget set by last year's budget.
  • An appetite statement with no number.
  • A risk estimate given as one value.

Where you have already met this

  • A heat map where everything is amber.
  • A control bought because a peer bought it.
  • A risk register updated once a year.
  • An appetite statement with no number in it.
